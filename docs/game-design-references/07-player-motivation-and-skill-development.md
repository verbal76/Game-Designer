# Player motivation and skill development

Sources: `VWWPP (1).pdf` and `DesigningVirtualWorlds.pdf` — Richard Bartle material on virtual worlds and why people play.

## Job of this lens
Use this material when Bob needs to reason about **why play is enjoyable, what the player is learning, and how player mastery develops**. It is especially useful for distinguishing player-skill progression from character-stat progression.

## Useful ideas
Bartle's work argues that simply calling a game “fun” is too vague for design reasoning. Designers should ask what the player finds enjoyable, why, how that changes with experience, and what the design does to support it.

A particularly useful development sequence is:
1. Discover the boundaries of possible actions.
2. Combine actions and learn what works.
3. Apply that knowledge toward successful play.
4. Internalize and master the skills.

This means a game can have meaningful progression even when the avatar never gains levels, stats, equipment, or permanent power.

Bartle's player-type work (including Achievers, Explorers, Socializers, and Killers and later refinements) can provide vocabulary for motivation, but it was developed around MUDs/virtual worlds.

## Bob rule
When the owner rejects character power progression, do not treat the game as missing progression. Ask whether progression is instead **player mastery, increasing challenge, new combinations of known actions, spatial advancement, discovery, or another form appropriate to the game**.

## Guardrails
- Do not force every player or game into Bartle player types.
- Do not universalize conclusions from MUDs/virtual worlds to all games.
- Use motivation models as hypotheses and lenses, not requirements.
- Never infer that an Achiever needs XP, that an Explorer needs collectibles, or that a Killer implies combat.
- Explicit owner intent outranks the model.
